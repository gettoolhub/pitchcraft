Data Slides That Don't Put People to Sleep

16 October 2026 · 4 min read

A founder sent me a deck last spring and said, "Our traction slide is strong, people love the numbers." I opened it. Eleven rows, six columns, 8-point font — a full profit-and-loss table pasted straight out of Excel. Nobody was loving those numbers, because nobody could read them.

That slide taught me something I keep seeing: most data slides aren't designed to be understood. They're designed to prove the data exists. The founder knows the numbers are good, so they show all of them, as if volume equals credibility. It doesn't. It equals a room full of glazed eyes.

One slide, one number, one story

Here's the thing most designers won't tell you: your audience can only take away a single message from a data slide. Not three insights. One. So decide what it is before you touch a chart. "Revenue tripled in eighteen months." "Customer churn dropped by half." Pick the sentence, then design the slide to make that sentence unavoidable.

Everything else goes. The y-axis labels that nobody reads, the legend with twelve colours, the data points from quarters nobody cares about. I once cut a client's fourteen-bar chart down to three bars — the only three quarters that mattered to the story — and their conversion on that deck went up. Not because of magic. Because the room finally got it.

People don't remember charts. They remember what the chart proved.

Three rules I never break

First: if the table has more than six cells visible, it's a document, not a slide. Summarise it into a visual. Second: kill the legend whenever you can — label the line or the bar directly. Legends force the eye to travel back and forth, and in a dark boardroom, that travel ends in confusion. Third: make the number big. Whatever the headline figure is, it should be the largest thing on the slide. The chart supports it; it doesn't replace it.

And one more, because I see it weekly: those default chart colours have to go. Excel blue and PowerPoint orange scream "I didn't care about this." You don't need fancy — one neutral grey for context, one accent for the thing you want people to see. Two colours. That's a whole data-design system.

Your data earned its place in the deck. Don't bury it in a spreadsheet wearing a costume. Give it room, give it a headline, and let the room actually feel what the numbers mean.
